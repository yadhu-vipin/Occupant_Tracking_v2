\begin{abstract}
A camera network that covers several buildings often has to find out which building a seen face belongs to.
A central gallery of face embeddings answers this, but it keeps sensitive biometric data in one place. We study
a routing step that avoids the central gallery. Each building publishes one Bloom filter built from
locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) codes of its own occupants. A captured face becomes $L$ codes that are checked
against all filters, and only the best-scoring buildings verify the face against their own reference embeddings.
On 500 CASIA-WebFace identities in 10 buildings, averaged over 20 random building assignments, the default setting
identifies <<abs_p3>>\% of queries correctly, against <<abs_cen>>\% for a centralized search with the same vote rule;
an adaptive rule that asks extra buildings only when the ranking is unclear reaches <<abs_ad>>\% and contacts
<<abs_adasked>> buildings on average. The mean face and the accept angle come from reference photographs only; $K$ and
$P$ were chosen after seeing test accuracy, so alternatives are reported. The real false-positive rate of the filters is <<abs_fpr>>\% instead of the configured 1\%; LSH code collisions
cause the difference. When the same people are regrouped into more and smaller buildings, the share of queries
whose true building is in the shortlist falls from <<abs_rec10>>\% at 10 buildings to <<abs_rec100>>\% at 100. Someone
who holds the filters can tell which building a person belongs to (AUC <<mem_auc3>>). Costs are computed, not measured; encryption and unknown visitors are not evaluated.

\keywords{Face identification \and Distributed systems \and Locality-sensitive hashing \and
Bloom filter \and Privacy in data analytics.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a face is seen at one building of a campus or company, the system often has to find out who the person is and
where that person is registered. The usual answer is one central gallery with the face embeddings of everyone. This
is simple and accurate, but it stores sensitive biometric data in a single place, which is an attractive target and
may conflict with data-minimization rules. The other extreme is a gallery in each building and a request to every
building for every query, which shows every query to every building.

We study the step between these two: a routing step that decides which few buildings should be asked. Each
building publishes one Bloom filter~\cite{bloom1970} built from LSH codes of its own occupants. A query is checked
against all filters, and only the best-scoring buildings receive it. Reference embeddings are not published. The
idea of publishing a compact summary of what each site holds, and checking the summaries before contacting a site,
is used in web cache sharing~\cite{fan2000}. Face matching is approximate, so the summary cannot hold exact keys;
it holds LSH codes instead.

\emph{Scope.} This paper is about the routing step. We measure how accurate it is, how many buildings it contacts,
how many false positives the filters give, and how it behaves when there are more buildings. All queries come from
enrolled people; identifying unknown visitors is left for future work. We do not implement or test any
cryptographic protection. We assume that messages between buildings use a standard authenticated, encrypted
channel, and Section~\ref{sec:model} states what is and is not protected. Storage and communication costs are
computed from message sizes; no distributed run was measured.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a routing design with one Bloom filter of LSH codes per building, and a way to choose the code length $K$ and
the number of codes $L$ from measured angle distributions;
\item measurements on 500 identities and 20 building assignments: accuracy against a centralized search, the gain
from multi-probe hashing, and a real false-positive rate of <<fpr_eff_mean>>\% that we trace to collisions between LSH codes;
\item a rule that asks extra buildings only when the two best scores are close, which is as accurate as asking all
shortlisted buildings and contacts about half as many;
\item an evaluation with up to 100 buildings, an analytic cost comparison, and a measurement of what the published
filters reveal about who is in a building.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\emph{Face identification with secure computation.} Erkin et al.~\cite{erkin2009} let one party with a face image
and another party with a database of face templates run Eigenfaces recognition, using homomorphic encryption and
secure multiparty computation, so that the database holder learns neither the image nor the result. Sadeghi et
al.~\cite{sadeghi2010} combine homomorphic encryption with garbled circuits and need fewer rounds, less
communication and less computation for the same task. These protocols protect one query
against one database holder. We address a different part of the problem: finding which of many sites holds the
person.

\emph{Biometric template protection and Bloom filters.} Jain et al.~\cite{jain2008} survey ways to protect stored
biometric templates. Rathgeb et al.~\cite{rathgeb2014} apply Bloom filters to iris templates. We use Bloom filters
differently: one filter summarizes all occupants of a building, and it is used to choose which building to ask.

\emph{Approximate nearest-neighbour search.} LSH~\cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}, multi-probe
LSH~\cite{lv2007}, product quantization~\cite{jegou2011} and GPU libraries~\cite{johnson2021} make search over large
galleries fast. They assume that one machine or one organization holds the whole gallery.

\emph{Bloom-filter summaries in distributed systems.} Summary Cache~\cite{fan2000} lets web proxies exchange Bloom
filters of their contents and ask only the proxies whose filter matches; Broder and Mitzenmacher~\cite{broder2004}
survey such uses. Our routing step follows this idea, with LSH codes in place of exact keys.

\emph{Camera networks.} Mohan and Menon~\cite{mohan2020} model the identification and tracking of occupants across
buildings as a distributed state transition system. We study how a query can be routed to the building that holds the
person. To our knowledge, the combination of LSH codes and per-building Bloom filters has not been measured for this
task before.

\section{System and Threat Model}
\label{sec:model}

There are $B$ buildings. Each building holds the reference embeddings of its $N=50$ occupants ($R=20$ photographs
each) and never publishes them. A query is a photograph of a person who is enrolled in exactly one building. We
assume that the buildings follow the protocol but may try to learn from what they see, and that messages between
buildings use an authenticated, encrypted channel. This channel is assumed; we do not implement or evaluate it.

\emph{What the design avoids.} No building publishes its reference embeddings, there is no gallery in one central
place, and no reference embedding is sent between buildings during routing or verification. We do not claim that the
published filters are irreversible or that they give biometric privacy.

\emph{What it does not protect.} A filter holds hashed LSH codes of the occupants, not their embeddings. Still,
anyone who holds the filters, the public hash parameters and a photograph of a person can compute the same routing
score that routing uses, and can therefore test whether that person belongs to a building; Section~\ref{sec:leak}
measures how well. This leakage is the signal that routing needs, so it cannot be removed without losing routing.
Guessing candidate faces and using outside information could leak more, and we did not study them. A shortlisted
building also receives the query embedding. Malicious or colluding buildings, attacks that guess embeddings from many
queries, and traffic analysis are outside the scope of this paper. In a full system, a building that confirms a
visitor may keep that person's reference embeddings for the length of the visit; this does share templates, it is
outside the scope of this paper, and it is not evaluated.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Embedding and Preprocessing}

Faces are detected with SCRFD-10GF~\cite{guo2022} and converted by ArcFace~\cite{deng2019} (InsightFace
\code{buffalo\_l}, pretrained and not trained by us) into a vector $\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}$, which is scaled
to unit length and called $x$. For unit vectors, $u\cdot v=\cos\theta$, so $\theta=\arccos(u\cdot v)$ is the angle
between two faces.

ArcFace embeddings lie in a narrow cone, so hyperplanes through the origin would put almost all of them on the same
side. We subtract the population mean $\bar{x}$ and scale to unit length again,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{pre}(x)=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{\lVert x-\bar{x}\rVert},
\end{equation}
using the same $\bar{x}$ at every building and for every query.

\subsection{Random-Hyperplane LSH}

Let $H$ be $K\cdot L$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{512}$ drawn independently from a standard Gaussian with a fixed seed
that all buildings share. Each vector $h$ gives one bit: $b=1$ if $x\cdot h\ge 0$, and $b=0$ otherwise. Groups of $K$
consecutive bits are packed into an integer,
\begin{equation}
  c_\ell=\sum_{i=0}^{K-1} b_{\ell,i}\,2^{i}\in[0,2^{K}),\qquad \ell=0,\dots,L-1,
\end{equation}
which gives $L$ codes per face. So that a match counts only within its own slot, each code is combined with its slot
number into an \emph{item}, $s_\ell=\ell\cdot 2^{K}+c_\ell$. This is the angular LSH family of
Charikar~\cite{charikar2002}: for unit vectors at angle $\theta$ (in degrees), a random hyperplane gives them the
same bit with probability
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{bit}}=1-\frac{\theta}{180}.
\end{equation}
We use this family, and not MinHash~\cite{broder1997} or bit sampling, because ArcFace embeddings are dense real
vectors that are compared by angle.

\subsection{Choosing $K$ and $L$}
\label{sec:kl}

Two codes match only if all $K$ bits agree, which has probability $p_{\text{bit}}^{K}$. We average this value over
the array of measured pair angles, separately for genuine pairs (same person) and impostor pairs (different people),
to get $p_{\text{code}}^{(G)}$ and $p_{\text{code}}^{(I)}$. The average is taken \emph{after} raising to the power
$K$. The function $x\mapsto x^{K}$ is convex, so pairs with small angles dominate the average; using the mean angle
first gives a value of $p_{\text{code}}^{(G)}$ that is too low. The $L$ codes use independent hyperplanes, so
\begin{equation}
  \text{recall}=1-\bigl(1-p_{\text{code}}^{(G)}\bigr)^{L},\qquad
  \text{FPR}_{\text{imp}}=1-\bigl(1-p_{\text{code}}^{(I)}\bigr)^{L},
\end{equation}
and for a recall target of $0.90$
\begin{equation}
  L=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1-0.90)}{\ln\bigl(1-p_{\text{code}}^{(G)}\bigr)}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
For each $K$ from 4 to 15 we solve for $L$, drop values of $K$ with $\text{FPR}_{\text{imp}}>0.10$, and keep the
smallest $K\!\cdot\!L$. The pairs are measured on reference photographs only (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). This model
picks $K=10$, $L=88$: for $K=10$ it gives $p_{\text{code}}^{(G)}=<<pg10>>$ and $\text{FPR}_{\text{imp}}=<<fpr10>>$, for
$K=11$ it needs $L=122$ ($<<pg11>>$ and $<<fpr11>>$), and for $K=13$ it needs $L=<<L13>>$ ($<<pg13>>$ and $<<fpr13>>$).
The model looks at each pair on its own, but a real filter holds 50 people, so we also run the full pipeline with the
model's choice and with $K=11$, $L=122$ (Section~\ref{sec:k1011}).

\subsection{Per-Building Bloom Filter}
\label{sec:bloom}

Each building publishes a Bloom filter of its occupants' codes. The 20 preprocessed reference embeddings of an
occupant are averaged and scaled to unit length to give one centroid, which is encoded into $L$ items. A building
therefore stores $n=N\cdot L=6100$ items at $L=122$. The filter size follows the standard formula for a target
false-positive rate $f^{\star}=0.01$,
\begin{equation}
  m=\left\lceil\frac{-n\ln f^{\star}}{(\ln 2)^{2}}\right\rceil=58{,}469,\qquad
  \kappa=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{m}{n}\ln 2\right)=7,
\end{equation}
which is about 7.3\,KB per building. The filter is always sized for $N=50$ occupants, so its length does not reveal
how many people a building really has.

Bit positions are computed with Kirsch--Mitzenmacher double hashing~\cite{kirsch2006}. Two hashes of the item, with
salts derived from the shared seed $\sigma$, are combined as
\begin{equation}
  h_1=\text{SM64}(s\oplus(2\sigma+1)),\quad h_2=\text{SM64}(s\oplus(2\sigma+2))\,\vee\,1,\quad
  p_j=\bigl((h_1+j\,h_2)\bmod 2^{64}\bigr)\bmod m,
\end{equation}
for $j=0,\dots,\kappa-1$, where SM64 is the SplitMix64 finalizer~\cite{steele2014}. The sum wraps around at 64 bits
before the reduction modulo $m$. Setting the lowest bit of $h_2$ makes it odd, so it is never zero and the $\kappa$
positions do not all land on the same bit. \textsc{Add} sets the $\kappa$ bits of an item, and \textsc{Check}
reports an item as present only if all $\kappa$ bits are set. There are no false negatives. We wrote the Bloom
filter and the LSH encoder ourselves in NumPy, without external hashing or Bloom-filter libraries.

\subsection{Multi-Probe Routing}
\label{sec:probe}

With a recall target of $0.90$, about one query in ten matches none of its own building's codes exactly, and the true
building falls out of the shortlist. When a code misses, it is usually because one bit flipped, and that bit is
typically the one whose projection $|x\cdot h|$ was closest to zero. Following multi-probe LSH~\cite{lv2007}, for
each slot we also look up the codes obtained by flipping, one at a time, each of the $P$ bits with the smallest
margin,
\begin{equation}
  s'_{\ell,j}=\ell\cdot2^{K}+\bigl(c_\ell\ \text{XOR}\ 2^{w_{\ell,j}}\bigr),\quad j=1,\dots,P,
\end{equation}
where $w_{\ell,j}$ is the index of the $j$-th weakest bit of slot $\ell$. This gives $L(1+P)$ lookups per filter.
The routing score of a building is the number of \emph{different slots} with at least one item present, so it stays
between 0 and $L$. The $S=5$ buildings with the highest scores form the shortlist, and ties are broken by building
order. The size of the shortlist is fixed.

\subsection{Verification and Policies}
\label{sec:vote}

A shortlisted building compares the query with its private reference embeddings. A reference \emph{votes} if its
angle to the query is at most $\tau$. The occupant with the most votes wins (ties go to the smallest single angle),
and the match is accepted only if the winner has at least $V=12$ of 20 votes. Otherwise the system abstains. The
accept angle $\tau$ is the cutoff that gives the smallest sum of the genuine false-reject rate and the impostor
false-accept rate. The centralized baseline applies the same vote to every enrolled occupant.

We use two ways to choose the final answer among the shortlisted buildings, and the results below always say which
one is used. In the \emph{default rule}, the winner is the occupant with the most votes among all occupants of the
$S$ shortlisted buildings, and ties go to the smallest angle. This rule gives the multi-probe results and the
headline number of the abstract. In the \emph{policies} (Section~\ref{sec:escalation}), buildings are asked one by
one, each asked building returns its own best occupant, and ties between buildings are decided by rank order.
\emph{Sequential}: ask the buildings in rank order and stop at the first one whose best vote reaches $V$; a wrong
building ranked above the true one can then confirm first. \emph{Query-all}: ask all $S$ buildings and take the
highest vote. \emph{Adaptive}: let $g$ be the difference between the two highest routing scores. If $g\le g_0$, the
ranking is unclear and all $S$ buildings are asked; otherwise the sequential rule is used.
\label{sec:escalation}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We use 500 identities of CASIA-WebFace~\cite{yi2014}, spread over $B=10$ buildings of 50 people. Each person has 20
enrolled reference photographs and 20 held-out test photographs, which gives 20,000 embeddings. Every one of the
10,000 test photographs is a query and is routed over all $B$ buildings. The evaluation is \emph{closed-set}: every
query belongs to an enrolled person. We use $K=11$, $L=122$, $P=3$, $S=5$, $V=12$ and $f^{\star}=0.01$.

\emph{Use of test photographs.} The mean face, the accept angle and the angle statistics behind $K$ and $L$ are
estimated from reference photographs only: the mean face is the mean of the 10,000 reference embeddings, genuine
pairs are two reference photographs of the same person, and impostor pairs are reference photographs of different
people. This gives $\tau=<<tau>>^{\circ}$. An earlier version of these experiments used test photographs for the mean
face and for $\tau$ ($80.54^{\circ}$). With the reference-only estimates, correct-identification rates changed by at
most 0.06 points, and misidentification rates fell by 0.1 to 0.15 points because $\tau$ is lower. The values $K=11$ and $P=3$ were
chosen after looking at test accuracy, so we report $K=10$, the choice of the model, next to $K=11$, and the whole
sweep over $P$ (Section~\ref{sec:k1011}).

\emph{Seeds.} Each result is repeated for 20 seeds. Seed $s$ changes three things together: the random assignment of
the 500 identities to buildings of 50, the LSH hyperplanes, and the Bloom filter salt. The embeddings, the mean face
and the accept angle stay the same, so the centralized baseline is a single number. We report the mean over seeds
with a 95\% confidence half-width ($\pm$, Student-$t$ with 19 degrees of freedom) and, for comparisons, the paired
difference over seeds. For $B=2$ and $B=5$ we take the identities of the first two and the first five buildings of
the original layout and regroup them into buildings of 50. A single run with the original assignment and hyperplane
seed 42 gave <<ship_p3>>\% at $P=3$ with the default rule, <<ship_diff>> points <<ship_dir>> the 20-seed mean; <<ship_ge>>
of the 20 seeds reach that value.

\emph{Metrics.} Every query ends in exactly one of four outcomes: \emph{correct}; \emph{misidentified} (a wrong
person is accepted, the most serious error); \emph{abstained-reject} (the true building was shortlisted but nobody
reached $V$ votes); and \emph{abstained-routing} (the true building was not shortlisted and no one reached $V$
votes). The centralized baseline gives <<cen_correct>>\% correct, <<cen_misid>>\% misidentified and <<cen_abst>>\%
abstained.

\subsection{Effective False-Positive Rate}
\label{sec:fpr}

The 1\% target describes the bit array for items that are independent of the stored ones. We measured the rate the
system really has by building all ten filters and testing the enrolled items of the \emph{other nine} buildings
against each one. The real per-item rate is $<<fpr_eff>>\%$ over the 20 seeds. When we compare the integer codes
directly, without a Bloom array, $<<coll>>\%$ of the items of other buildings already equal an enrolled item in the
same slot. This is close to the <<uni>>\% expected if the codes of 50 occupants were evenly spread over
$2^{11}=2048$ values, namely $1-(1-2^{-K})^{N}$. The Bloom array alone, tested on random integers that were never
inserted, gives $<<bloom>>\%$ at <<fill>>\% fill, close to its design rate. The extra false positives therefore come
from the LSH codes, not from the Bloom array.

A larger $K$ gives more possible codes and lowers the real false-positive rate, but $L$ must grow to keep recall at
0.90. At $K=13$, $L=<<L13>>$ each filter grows from 7.3\,KB to about <<size13>>\,KB and the real rate falls to
<<fpr13_eff>>\%. With $P=6$ it is $<<k13_k11p7>>$ points more accurate than the best $K=11$ setting ($P=7$,

default (<<k13_corr>>\% against <<p3_corr>>\%; better in <<k13_k11p3_wins>> of 20 seeds), but it needs <<look13>> lookups

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

The cost figures are computed from message sizes and from the measured number of buildings asked; they are not
measurements. We did not measure latency, throughput or traffic in a distributed run, because all calls were
in-process. A query embedding has 512 single-precision values, which is 2,048 bytes. Routing replaces a 20.5\,MB
gallery in one place with ten published filters of 7.3\,KB each (the buildings keep 2.05\,MB of private embeddings
each). A query then costs <<seq_kb>>\,KB with sequential asking (<<seq_asked>> buildings), <<ad_kb>>\,KB with the
adaptive rule (<<ad_asked>> buildings) and 10.2\,KB when all five shortlisted buildings are asked, against 20.5\,KB
when all ten buildings are asked and 2.0\,KB for a central gallery. Each query still costs $B\cdot L(1+P)=4{,}880$
filter lookups, a cost that grows linearly with $B$.

\subsection{What the Filters Reveal}
\label{sec:leak}

We measured the simplest attack. Someone who holds all published filters and the public hash parameters scores a
photograph against every filter, in the same way as routing does. With $P=3$, this score separates the occupants of a
building from everyone else with an AUC of <<mem_auc3>> (<<mem_auc0>> without probing), averaged over buildings and
10 seeds, and the highest-scoring building is the person's own building for <<mem_top3>>\% of the photographs
(<<mem_top0>>\% without probing). The filters therefore leak which building a person belongs to. This is not a way to
recover a face, but it is a real leak, and it grows with the accuracy of the routing itself. We did not test attacks
that use many queries, candidate guessing or outside information, so we make no claim of biometric privacy.

\subsection{More Buildings}
\label{sec:scale}

The corpus has only 500 identities, so a larger network can only be built with smaller buildings. We regroup the same
500 people into $B=10$, 25, 50 and 100 buildings of 50, 20, 10 and 5 people. Each filter is sized for its own number
of occupants, so the total size of all filters stays the same (about 73\,KB). We use 2,000 queries per seed (4
photographs per person) and 10 seeds. Table~\ref{tab:scale} shows that the share of queries whose true building is in

and a fixed set of people; they do not replace a test with a larger population.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

\emph{Limitations.} All results use the same 500 identities and photographs from one public dataset; the intervals
describe the effect of building assignments and hash randomness, not a different population. The values $K=11$ and
$P=3$ were chosen after seeing test accuracy, and we report the alternatives next to them. CASIA-WebFace photographs
come from the web and not from surveillance cameras, so the absolute accuracy is probably higher than it would be in
practice. The ArcFace model was pretrained on a large public face collection, and we did not check how much it overlaps
with CASIA-WebFace. The largest network has 100 small buildings. The evaluation is closed-set, so we did not measure
what happens when a person who is enrolled nowhere, such as a visitor, is seen. The vote rule ($\tau$ and $V=12$) was
chosen for enrolled people, and a first test with held-out people showed that it can accept a noticeable share of
them, so the acceptance rule has to be studied and tightened before the system is used with unknown visitors. Storage
and communication costs are computed from message sizes; we did not measure latency or traffic in a distributed run.
We did not implement or evaluate the encrypted channel, the visit-length storage of templates or attacks on the
filters beyond the membership test of Section~\ref{sec:leak}. A Bloom filter cannot delete items, so a change in
enrollment needs a rebuild.

\emph{Conclusion.} Per-building Bloom filters of LSH codes can route face queries to the right building without a
central gallery. With multi-probe hashing the default setting identifies <<abs_p3>>\% of queries correctly at 10
buildings against <<abs_cen>>\% for a centralized search, and the adaptive rule reaches <<abs_ad>>\% while contacting
about <<abs_adasked>> buildings. The cost is lost recall that grows with the number of buildings, and a false-positive
rate that LSH collisions raise well above the Bloom filter's design value. The privacy conclusions are limited to what
was evaluated: the design avoids publishing reference embeddings and a central gallery, and it leaks which building a
person belongs to (AUC <<mem_auc3>>); encryption, stronger attacks and template storage during visits were not
evaluated. The next steps are a larger and more varied population, the identification of unknown visitors with a
stricter acceptance rule, and measurements in a distributed deployment.
